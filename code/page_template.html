Every Tour de France, mapped

All {{source.men_stages|int}} stages of the men's Tour from {{source.first_year|raw}} to {{source.last_year|raw}}, and all {{source.women_stages|int}} of the Tour de France Femmes since {{source.women_first|raw}}, drawn as lines from start to finish. How the race wandered: from a giant loop round the edge of France to Grand Départs in Dublin, Florence and Copenhagen. The towns it keeps coming back to, how far it strays abroad, and how it got shorter and faster.

The first Tour de France, in July 1903, had six stages. Riders left Paris for Lyon, then rode on to Marseille, Toulouse, Bordeaux and Nantes, and back to Paris: {{dist.y1903|int}} km in all, an average of {{dist.avg_stage_max|int}} km a stage. The race has been held {{source.men_years|int}} times, with breaks only for the two world wars, and every stage is recorded on the official Tour website. Thomas Camminady has gathered those pages into LeTourDataSet, a free table of every stage and every rider, and it is the source for everything here.

The table gives each stage's start and finish town, but not where they are. So I matched every name against GeoNames, a free gazetteer of the world's towns, and drew each stage as a straight line from start to finish. The result, below, is a portrait of more than a century of the race. Press play to watch each Tour drawn stage by stage in yellow, or click any town to see every stage it has hosted.

    02 · The giant loopRound the edge, then across the middle

    For its first half century the Tour was a tour in the literal sense: a lap of France. It ran round the coasts and frontiers, from the Channel to the Atlantic, over the Pyrenees, along the Mediterranean, up the Alps and across the north. In the 1920s, {{shape.edge50_1920s|int}}% of the French stage towns it used lay within 50 km of the coast or a land border, although that strip makes up only {{shape.area_edge50_pct|int}}% of the country. In the 2020s the figure is {{shape.edge50_2020s|int}}%.

    The early loop was also unbroken. Through the 1910s, 1920s and 1930s every stage began in the town where the last one had finished, so the route could be drawn without lifting the pen. That changed slowly after the war, then quickly. In the 1960s, {{shape.transfer_pct_1960s|int}}% of stages began somewhere else; in the 2020s, {{shape.transfer_pct_2020s|int}}% do, and the riders and their convoy are moved by bus, train or plane. Summed as straight lines, the gaps between finishes and next starts now add up to about {{shape.transfer_km_2020s|int}} km a Tour. The longest single jump in the data is {{shape.transfer_top.0.km|int}} km, from {{shape.transfer_top.0.from|raw}} in the Basque Country to {{shape.transfer_top.0.to|raw}} near Paris in {{shape.transfer_top.0.year|raw}}.

    03 · Shorter and fasterFrom {{dist.longest_km|int}} km to {{dist.y2026|int}}

    The longest Tour was the {{dist.longest_year|raw}} edition: {{dist.longest_km|int}} km, in a decade when the race averaged {{dist.dist_1920s|int}} km and its stages {{dist.avg_stage_1920s|int}} km. The winner, Lucien Buysse, was on his bike for {{dist.time_1926_h|int}} hours. The {{source.last_year|raw}} Tour covered {{dist.y2026|int}} km in {{dist.y2026_n|int}} stages, {{dist.dist_drop_pct|int}}% less, with stages averaging {{dist.avg_stage_2026|int}} km, and its winner needed {{dist.time_2026_h|int}} hours.

    Total distance and the winner's speed, every Tour

    Bars: the official total distance of each Tour, coloured by decade.     The riders also got much faster. Maurice Garin won the first Tour at {{dist.speed_1903|f1}} km/h. The winners of the 1950s averaged {{dist.speed_1950s|f1}} km/h, and those of the 2020s {{dist.speed_2020s|f1}} km/h; the fastest of all was Tadej Pogačar in {{dist.speed_max_year|raw}}, at {{dist.speed_max|f2}} km/h. The data cannot say why. What it does show is a gap: the official record has no winner at all for 1999 to 2005, because those seven titles were stripped and never given to anyone else.

    The source has no length for single stages, only each Tour's total. As the crow flies, the longest stages were the opening legs of 1903 and 1904, Paris to Lyon, {{dist.crow_top.0.km|int}} km in a straight line; the road was longer still. Across all stages with two located towns, the straight line from start to finish averages {{dist.crow_mean|int}} km, and {{dist.loops|int}} stages, mostly time trials and circuits, started and finished in the same town.

    04 · Stage townsParis, Bordeaux, Pau

    The {{source.men_stages|int}} stages of the men's Tour used {{towns.men_n|int}} different towns, {{towns.once|int}} of them ({{towns.once_pct|int}}%) only once. Paris is in a class of its own: {{towns.paris.visits|int}} starts and finishes in {{towns.paris.tours|int}} Tours. Every Tour has ended in Paris except {{towns.final_elsewhere.0.0|raw}}, when the Olympic Games were in the capital and the race finished in {{towns.final_elsewhere.0.1|raw}}, and {{towns.paris_gd|int}} began there, including every Tour until the first Grand Départ elsewhere, in Évian in {{towns.gd_not_paris_first|raw}}. The next two are the gateways to the Pyrenees: Bordeaux, with {{towns.top_visits.1.n|int}} starts and finishes, and Pau, with {{towns.top_visits.2.n|int}}, the most common start of all ({{towns.top_starts.0.n|int}}). Luchon, at the foot of the passes in the central Pyrenees, is fourth.

    05 · Abroad{{abroad.gd_abroad|int}} Grand Départs outside France

    The Tour first crossed a border in {{abroad.first_abroad.year|raw}}, with a stage from {{abroad.first_abroad.start|raw}} to {{abroad.first_abroad.end|raw}}. The first Grand Départ abroad came in {{abroad.gd_first_abroad|raw}}, in {{abroad.gd_first_abroad_town|raw}}, and since then the race has started in {{abroad.gd_by_country.Netherlands|int}} Dutch towns, {{abroad.gd_by_country.Belgium|int}} Belgian, {{abroad.gd_by_country.Germany|int}} German and {{abroad.gd_by_country.Spain|int}} Spanish, as well as in Luxembourg, Switzerland, Monaco, Ireland, Denmark, Italy and, twice, England. Starting abroad has become the norm: {{abroad.gd_since2000|int}} of the {{abroad.tours_since2000|int}} Tours since 2000 began outside France.

    06 · DépartementsEvery one, at least once

    Has the Tour been everywhere? Using the département GeoNames gives each French stage town, every one of the {{deps.n|int}} départements of metropolitan France has hosted at least one start or finish. The busiest is Pyrénées-Atlantiques, home to Pau, Bayonne and the Aubisque, with {{deps.top.0.2|int}} starts and finishes in {{deps.top.0.3|int}} Tours, ahead of Haute-Garonne, which holds Luchon ({{deps.top.1.2|int}}). The least visited are Deux-Sèvres, where only Niort (in 1950) appears among the located towns, and the two départements of Corsica, which waited until the 100th Tour in 2013. Turn on "Départements" in Map layers to shade them by visits. The data has no record of the towns a stage merely passed through, so this counts only starts and finishes.

    07 · WinnersFrance {{winners.france|int}}, Belgium {{winners.belgium|int}}, Spain {{winners.spain|int}}

    LeTourDataSet's rider file lists the final classification of every Tour. It has no nationality column, so I added the nationality of each of the {{winners.n_riders|int}} overall winners by hand, and checked it against the national team that the file names for the {{winners.checked_by_team|int}} winners of the years when the Tour was raced by national teams. French riders won {{winners.france|int}} Tours, the last in {{winners.france_last|raw}}, by Bernard Hinault. Belgium has {{winners.belgium|int}}, Spain {{winners.spain|int}}, Italy {{winners.italy|int}}, Britain {{winners.uk|int}}, and Luxembourg and Slovenia {{winners.lux|int}} each. The first winner from outside France was François Faber of Luxembourg in {{winners.first_non_fr|raw}}, and the first from outside Europe Greg LeMond of the United States in {{winners.first_outside_europe|raw}}. Five men have won five times: Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault, Miguel Indurain and Tadej Pogačar.

    09 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. LeTourDataSet (commit {{source.commit|raw}}) holds {{source.men_rows|int}} stage rows for the men's Tour and {{source.women_rows|int}} for the women's. I merged {{source.joint|int}} stages listed twice because two riders shared the win, and dropped {{source.dropped_2022|int}} rows from 2022: a repeat of the opening time trial and two rows for the transfer from Denmark. That leaves {{source.men_stages|int}} men's stages (including {{source.prologues|int}} prologues and {{source.half_stages|int}} half stages, when a day was split in two) and {{source.women_stages|int}} women's. Distances are each Tour's official total; the winner's speed is that total divided by the winner's time.

    Places. Every start and finish name was matched to GeoNames cities1000, a list of {{source.gazetteer_rows|int}} places with at least 1,000 people, searching only the {{source.gazetteer_searched|int}} in the thirteen countries the Tour has visited. Names were compared without accents, hyphens or case, with St spelt out. A hand-made table of {{geo.alias_n|int}} names covers French names of foreign towns (Londres, Gand, Bâle), short and old names (Luchon, Les Sables), districts and venues inside a town (Paris Champs-Élysées, Futuroscope), and ski stations named after their village (Alpe d'Huez to Huez). Where a name fits several places, I used the one nearest the stage's other end, preferring France. In all {{geo.matched|int}} of {{geo.ends|int}} starts and finishes ({{geo.rate|f1}}%) are located, and {{geo.names_matched|int}} of {{geo.names|int}} distinct names. The {{geo.unmatched_names|int}} names left unmatched are mostly summit finishes and ski stations with no village in their name (the Puy de Dôme {{geo.unmatched_top.0.2|int}} times, Mont Ventoux {{geo.unmatched_top.1.2|int}}, Hautacam, the Plateau de Beille), lakes and venues spread across several villages, and villages too small for the gazetteer. They are listed in the data files. Mountain finishes are therefore under-drawn, especially since the 1970s.

    What the lines are. Each stage is a straight line between two towns. It shows where a stage went, not the road it took, so it understates distance and smooths every climb. Transfers are measured the same way. "Abroad" means a stage town outside France, including Monaco and Andorra. Départements come from the GeoNames code of each French stage town; a stage that only passed through a département does not count.
